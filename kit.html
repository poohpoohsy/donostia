The only genuinely hard one

Wednesday 7 October, the Grand Balcon Nord. Seven kilometres, four hours with six people, rough ground and short scrambling sections. Everything in the "must" column below exists because of this day.

The Swiss walks are wide, graded paths. The Basque ones are streets and a made staircase. Only Wednesday is a mountain trail.

Why, item by item

The reasoning

Hiking shoes Four days

Friday 2, Saturday 3, Wednesday 7, Thursday 8. Broken in, with grip and ankle support.

Wednesday is the one that decides it — rough ground and scrambling on a four-hour traverse. Friday matters for a different reason: the Trümmelbach staircase is wet rock, and smooth soles there are genuinely unsafe.

Saturday's Panorama Trail is wide and flat, so trainers would survive it, but October ground gets damp and it's a long day. In the Basque country, leave them at the hotel — San Antón is a made path and the rest is streets.

Thick socks Every mountain day

Several pairs, not one. The single best blister prevention there is, and blisters on Wednesday would ruin Thursday.

They also do a second job at altitude: on Sunday and Tuesday you're standing still in sub-zero air, and cold reaches feet before anywhere else.

Hiking poles One day, really

Wednesday 7 only. Rough descending ground over four hours is exactly what poles are for — they take load off knees and give you a third point of contact on the scrambling bits.

Everywhere else they'd be dead weight. The Swiss trails are graded and gentle; poles there are something to carry, not something to use.

The honest question: is one day worth packing them for? If anyone has knee trouble, yes, unambiguously. If not, collapsible ones that fit inside a daypack are the compromise — full-length poles are awkward on easyJet, where you get no cabin bag at all on the 9th.

Sunscreen Higher matters more than hotter

This is the one people get wrong, because they associate sunscreen with heat and Sunday at Jungfraujoch is minus ten.

UV rises roughly 10% for every 1,000m of altitude, and snow reflects up to 80% of it back at you. At 3,842m on Tuesday, standing on a terrace surrounded by glacier, you are getting sun from above and below at once — in air cold enough that you'll feel nothing until that evening.

High SPF, and SPF lip balm too. Lips burn badly at altitude and nobody packs for it.

Two different hats Not the same object

These get conflated and they shouldn't be.

A peaked sun cap for Saturday, Wednesday and Thursday — long days in open sun at 2,000–2,500m, where the peak also keeps glare off your face for photographs.

A warm hat for Sunday and Tuesday. At −10 to −15°C with wind on an exposed terrace, a cap does nothing. This is a beanie that covers your ears.

Two small items. Both worth the space.

Neck gaiter · 頸巾 The most useful thing on this list

A tube-style gaiter — a Buff or similar — rather than a wrapped scarf. It's the highest-value item per gram on the whole trip because it does four jobs:

Neck warmth at altitude. Pull it over your face against wind on the Aiguille du Midi terraces, where the wind is the thing that actually drives people back inside. Sun protection for the back of the neck on Wednesday, which burns and nobody notices. And a headband if it's warmer than expected.

A wrapped scarf does one of those and gets in the way of a rucksack strap. Bring the tube version.

Sunglasses Not optional at altitude

Snow and glacier glare is severe enough at 3,400m+ that this stops being about comfort. Sunday and Tuesday particularly.

They also make the difference between seeing the view and squinting at it — which sounds trivial until you've paid CHF 89 to be there.

Gloves Sunday and Tuesday

Below freezing on both summit days. And there's a practical consequence beyond comfort: you can't work a phone with numb hands. Thin liner gloves that work with a touchscreen, or gloves you can pull off quickly.

Waterproof jacket Two very different reasons

Friday 2: you get genuinely soaked climbing the staircase behind Staubbach and again inside Trümmelbach. This isn't rain protection, it's spray protection, and it's certain rather than possible.

Wednesday 7: four hours exposed on a traverse where the weather can change. Also the Basque country in October, which rains.

Daypack, 20–25L Sat 3 and Wed 7

Wednesday needs it — everyone carries their own packed lunch, snacks and water, and there's no refill point on the balcony. Saturday needs it for layers you'll take on and off between 1,034m and 2,343m.

Water bottle Every day

Swiss tap water and village fountains are excellent and free. Altitude also dehydrates you faster than you expect, which is half of why people get headaches up there.

Power bank Sun 4, Tue 6, Wed 7

Cold flattens phone batteries fast. On the Sphinx terrace a half-charged phone can die outright.

Keep the phone in an inside pocket rather than an outer one — body heat is the real fix, the power bank is the backup.

Small things, easy to forget

Worth adding to the list

Blister plasters. Compeed or similar. Four hours on rough ground with six people means somebody will need one, and Thursday depends on Wednesday not wrecking anyone's feet.

A dry bag or ziplock for your phone. Friday, for Trümmelbach. Water gets everywhere in there.

A lens cloth. Cable car glass and cold air both fog things, and every photograph on Sunday and Tuesday goes through one or the other.

SPF lip balm. Listed above but worth repeating — it's the thing nobody packs and everybody regrets.

What you don't need

Crampons, ice axes, technical gear. Nothing on this trip requires them. The Grand Balcon Nord is a hiking trail, not a glacier route.

Boots in the Basque week. Days 9 to 15 are city streets, a beach, and one made staircase up Monte Urgull. Comfortable walking shoes are correct.
